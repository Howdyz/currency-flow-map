Reading the map
What is being measured?

Every year the IRS matches tax returns to see who changed states and counties. Each arrow is the adjusted gross income of the households that moved along that route between one filing year and the next. It's money that follows people: paychecks, business income and retirement income that now gets earned and spent somewhere else. The year slider covers 2011–12 through 2022–23, the latest the IRS has published.

#1 industry is the sector with the most private-sector jobs in each county, from the Census Bureau's County Business Patterns.

How to use it

Click any state to see where its money went and where its newcomers came from, then click one of its counties for that county's own routes and top industry. Orange arrows leave, green arrows arrive. Drag to pan, scroll or pinch to zoom, press space to blink the heat layer, and press ▶ to play through the years. Table ranks every state, route and county, and Share copies a link that reopens exactly what you're looking at.

What it leaves out

Only households that filed taxes both years are counted, so it misses people who don't file and money that moves without anyone moving (online sales, investments, federal spending). For each state the map keeps its six biggest routes in and out, and for each county its eight biggest; the IRS doesn't list county routes with fewer than 20 households. Industry counts leave out government and most farm jobs. Connecticut has reported by planning region since 2022, which the county shapes here predate.
